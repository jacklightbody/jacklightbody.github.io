A lot of people hate taxes.

It sucks to lose a chunk of your paycheck before you even see it. And it's even worse to spend hours proving that you shouldn't have had more taken out. 

But for many people I suspect the worst part is the complexity. The thousands of rules in the tax code leads to a constant nagging feeling that you're making a mistake, or that someone else is gaming the system and getting a better deal. But digging in would take hours so most people just guess, submit, and live with it.

This isn't anything new. We've had taxes since [ancient Mesopotamia 4,000 years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bala_taxation). People get over it. Yes, you lose some money, but you get neat things like hospitals and roads and schools in exchange. 

But it's a balance. People demand change when the trade stops making sense. British taxation led to "No taxation without representation" and the founding of our country. When California property tax bills doubled in the 1970s because of high inflation, nearly two-thirds of California voters opted to pass Proposition 13 and cut property tax receipts by about 60% overnight. 

We may be entering another of those periods. About [60% of Americans now think their tax bill is too high](https://news.gallup.com/poll/707951/americans-tax-views-remain-negative.aspx). There's a growing movement to cut or eliminate property taxes: Ohio activists are petitioning to abolish them, Florida voters just [raised the homestead exemption](https://www.ncsl.org/news/details/landmark-tax-decisions-feature-in-2026-midterm-elections), and Tennessee voters eliminated the state property tax. And only [17% of Americans trust the Federal government to do the right thing most of the time](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/12/04/public-trust-in-government-1958-2025/). 

Pressure is ramping. And if the government doesn't do something to release it, others will. 

A union in California recently proposed a one-time 5% wealth tax on billionaires that would have been the first of its kind in the country. The establishment, including Gov. Gavin Newsom, opposed it, [and the bill was heavily criticized as poorly designed with high administrative costs and uncertain revenue](https://thedailyeconomy.org/article/californias-billionaire-tax-may-cost-more-than-it-raises-reports-find/). Yet the idea that wealthy people are gaming the system and not paying their fair share resonated with [more than 1.5 million people](https://fortune.com/2026/04/28/california-billionaire-tax-makes-november-ballot-wealth-inequality/), enough to force it onto the ballot.

The California measure ended up [voted down, 52.8% to 47.2%](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/node/437596), but the union has [vowed to bring it back](https://edsource.org/california-prop-40-billionaire-tax-has-failed-backers-vow-to-try-again). Will it lose again? Will Ohio's property tax abolition? Once these things are on the ballot, nobody can say. Politicians would be wise to get ahead of it and make the trade fair again rather than waiting to be forced.
